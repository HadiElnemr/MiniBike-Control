\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Bicycle riding is a frequent human activity and an acquired skill, but creating a controller that can steady a bicycle on its own is a difficult engineering task.
\gls{mpc} and \gls{lqr}, two examples of traditional model-based controllers, depend on a precise system model.
However, certain aspects of a bicycle, like as the centre of gravity, can be challenging to find and may change as a result of different loads or extra passengers.
By solving a \gls{sdp}, a data-driven state-feedback controller is used in this research effort to stabilise a self-balancing minibike.
Instead of identifying the bicycle's system model, the method designs the controller using noisy data that has been obtained.
To test and evaluate the controller, two bicycle models are used.
Using noisy data produced based on the system dynamics, simulations and experiments on a model-scale bicycle show good stabilisation of the system.

To balance a bicycle, a torque should be generated to prevent falling on its side due to gravity. To achieve this, different techniques for torque generation can be introduced such as adding a device to control the leaning, or by controlling the steering angle of the bicycle while moving. While the bicycle is stationary, a flywheel can generate the required torque to achieve the balancing \cite{kawaguchi2011}. Another approach is by using a \gls{cmg} which was tested out by He and Zhao \cite{he2015}. Since controlling the steering angle to produce the balancing torque does not need additional devices, it was considered by numerous researchers and tested against emerging control algorithms. Balancing using the steering is based on steering into the direction of falling. A drawback of using only the steering angle is that the bicycle needs an initial speed before being able to balance.

This research project is an attempt to apply the theoretical data-driven min-max MPC developed by Xie in \cite{xie2024}.
The considered application is a model-scale bicycle at the \gls{IST}, University of Stuttgart.
The bicycle was chosen as the IST has a rear-steered bicycle which is a more complex system that could be tested against the results achieved from this project.
In the following sections, the bicycle modelling and data-driven MPC are discussed.

\section{Bicycle Modelling}

Before implementing a control strategy for balancing a bicycle, a rigorous system model must be established for most techniques, though some approaches are model-free. System models can be classified as either kinematic or dynamic. The kinematic model does not consider the applied forces on the system, while the dynamic model considers the torques and forces interacting with the system. Researchers set different parameters for the dynamic models depending on the bicycle under study. Some parameters could be neglected to simplify the system. Complex systems emerging from accurate modelling demand sophisticated control algorithms, which require more computation power. Bicycles are non-linear and inherently statically unstable, similar to an inverted pendulum. Examples of dynamic models for the bicycle include the Whipple model \cite{whipple1899stability} and the point mass model \cite{limebeer2006}.


%\subsection{Linear-Parameter-Varying (LPV) Models}
One of the primary methods used in bicycle modelling is the \gls{lpv} approach, which accounts for variations in parameters, such as velocity, that affect the stability of the bicycle. The \gls{lpv} framework is particularly useful when the bicycle's speed is subject to change, as it enables the controller to adapt to varying conditions.

In contrast to kinematic models, kinetic models focus on the fundamental forces and torques on the system. Moreover, software tools for modelling with a multibody approach is used in research such as in \cite{tapken2018modelling}.

\section{Bicycle Control}
For controlling bicycles, several different approaches were studied by researchers.
Andersson et al. \cite{andersson2019} proposed a robust \gls{pid} using loop shaping to control a bicycle based on the point mass model.
Cerone et al. \cite{cerone2010StabilizationOA} proposed an \gls{lpv}-based stabilisation method for riderless bicycles, which uses a parameter-varying approach to handle the dynamic changes in speed. The study demonstrated that stability can be maintained between two specific speeds by discretising the continuous-time system, thereby addressing the nonlinearity inherent in the bicycle dynamics.
Tapken et al. \cite{tapken2018modelling} studied modelling the kinetic and dynamic aspects of bicycles, employing tools such as CAD modelling and Simscape Multibody to simulate and control the motion of a real mini-bike.
Since researchers neglect some parameters in the bicycle models to simplify computations, some uncertainties arise. Therefore, a robust controller can mitigate the errors generated by such models. Robust controllers include the \gls{smc}, which was proposed by Chen and Dao \cite{chen2010}, and the \gls{lqr}, which was proposed by Anjumol and Jisha \cite{anjumol2014}. The controllers proposed in the literature are largely tested in simulations rather than on a real bicycle. This leads to ignoring the emerging uncertainties from real-life models.
%
In \cite{persson2021comparative}, Persson et al. conducted a comparative analysis of various control models applied to a driverless bicycle. They studied different PID controllers, an LQR controller, in addition to a Fuzzy controller.
%
Motion planning for a bicycle was investigated in \cite{turnwald2019} by solving an optimisation problem, where a constrained Lagrangian is used to model the bicycle as a nonlinear system.
A comparative study by Yu et al. \cite{yu2018} investigated the effectiveness of steering control at low speeds, while studying the effect of different trail lengths that introduces the castor effect on the system.

\section{Data-Driven Control Methods}

Data-driven control techniques have emerged as a result of autonomous systems' dynamic and complicated character. These techniques use measurement data directly to infer control actions, without the need for accurate system modelling. When there are unknown process disturbance or model flaws, data-driven approaches are especially beneficial.

%\subsection{Data-Driven Model Predictive Control (MPC)}
One popular method for managing systems with limitations is MPC. Conventional MPC predicts system behaviour using a mathematical model. To reduce modelling errors, data-driven MPC can use input-output data directly to predict future states.

A developed formulation is the data-driven min-max MPC, which aims to robustly stabilise the system while minimizing the worst-case cost. In \cite{xie2024}, Xie et al. proposed a robust min-max MPC framework that, using noisy input-state data and formulating the control problem as an SDP, guarantees robust stability and constraint satisfaction while reducing conservatism compared to traditional data-driven MPC approaches. Moreover, in \cite{xie2024robust}, an adaptive scheme for implementing the online data-driven min-max MPC is developed. This is achieved by integrating new data which reduces the model uncertainty over time. The min-max optimisation along with the online adaptation is proved to enhance the robustness.
%\subsection{\improvement{Remove header and comparison}{Comparative Analysis of Data-Driven MPC Methods}}
A significant advantage of data-driven MPC is its ability to handle uncertainties without requiring an explicit system model. Compared to classical model-based MPC, data-driven methods are more adaptable when the system's dynamics change. Additionally, the use of online data in adaptive MPC helps reduce conservatism, achieving better closed-loop performance \cite{xie2024}.

\section{WHEELTEC MiniBike}
In this research project, a self-balancing mini-bike developed by \textbf{WHEELTEC} was utilized as the experimental platform.
The goal is to implement a data-driven State-Feedback controller for stabilising the bike.
The mini-bike is equipped with various sensors and actuators to enable control and data acquisition.
An STM32 Micro-controller is used to control the bike, where the C++ codes used to produce the Hexadecimal file to be burnt on the controller are given with the bicycle.

The bike achieves self-balancing through controlling the steering angle. This is done through a controller computed using \gls{lqr} combined with a \gls{pid} controller. The \gls{lqr} calculates optimal control inputs based on the state feedback to minimize the cost function, while the PID controller fine-tunes the steering speed by adjusting the PWM signals to the servo motor.

While moving with a constant speed, the system's balance is continuously monitored through the gyroscope, which provides real-time data on tilt angles and angular velocities, along with the steering angle.
These values are processed by the microcontroller to compute the necessary adjustments to the steering servo to maintain balance.
